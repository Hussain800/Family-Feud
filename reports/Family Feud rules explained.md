# Family Feud: Complete Rules for Beginners and Live-Event Organisers

## The One-Minute Summary

**Family Feud** is a game show where two families compete to guess the most popular answers to survey questions. A 100-person survey gave answers to each question; those answers are ranked by how many people said them. Each correct guess wins points equal to the number of people who gave that answer. The first family to reach 300 points wins the main game. Four rounds are played; if neither family reaches 300 points after four rounds, one final sudden-death question (worth triple value, only the #1 answer on the board) decides the winner—the first family to buzz in with that top answer wins the game and plays Fast Money, a bonus round where two players answer five rapid-fire questions to win up to $20,000. The host asks questions, keeps score, and judges whether answers are close enough to the board.

---

## Who Is Who: The Roles

**The Host** reads the questions out loud, announces the survey answers when they're revealed, watches for buzzer timing, makes judgment calls on whether answers are acceptable, tracks the score, keeps the game moving at a fun pace, and provides commentary and entertainment. Think of the host as both the referee and the person keeping everyone energized. (On TV, Steve Harvey is the host; in a live event, you'll pick someone confident and comfortable on stage.)

**Two Families of Five** (on TV) or **Two Teams of any size** (at a live event) compete against each other. Each family member gets a turn to answer during the round.

**The Scorekeeper** (at a live event, not visible on TV) keeps track of each team's total points, strike count, and which answers have been used.

**The Answer-Board Operator** (at a live event) controls the display of answers—either by revealing them on a physical board or managing a digital screen.

**The Timekeeper** (optional, especially for Fast Money) makes sure time limits are respected.

**The Judge or Dispute Resolver** (optional but helpful for large events) settles arguments over whether an answer is close enough to an answer on the board.

## The Game, Step by Step: The Main Rounds

### The Survey Behind Every Question

Before the game is even filmed, the show asks **100 random people** the same question. Their answers are collected and ranked by how many people gave each answer. The most popular answers go on the game board, and each is worth points equal to the number of people who said it. 

**Example:** If 100 people are asked "Name a fruit," and the results are:
- Apple: 35 people → worth 35 points
- Banana: 28 people → worth 28 points
- Orange: 22 people → worth 22 points

Those three answers go on the board (along with others). If only one person said "Kiwi," it doesn't appear on the board, because answers need at least 2 people to count.

### The Board

The board is where all the answers are displayed, either as a physical poster, a digital screen, or a whiteboard. The host shows the category (e.g., "Name a fruit") and the answers are hidden at first, then revealed one by one as the families guess them. The point value of each answer is shown next to it.

### The Face-Off: How Every Round Starts

At the start of each round, one person from each family stands at a podium. The host asks a survey question. **Both players race to buzz in first.** The first contestant to buzz in gives an answer.

- **If that answer is #1 (the most popular answer on the board):** That family immediately controls the round and plays next. The other family becomes the "steal team" and waits.
- **If the answer is not #1:** The opponent gets a chance to answer. Whoever's answer scores higher points wins control. If the answers tie on points, whoever answered first wins control.
- **If neither answer is on the board or they do not answer within three seconds:** The remaining 8 family members (or however many remain on your teams) take turns answering one at a time, alternating from each side, until someone gives an answer on the board.

### Play or Pass: The Strategic Choice

After the face-off, the family that wins control chooses to **play** or **pass**. 
- **Play:** The family stays in control and answers questions to the same topic.
- **Pass:** The family gives up control to the other family.

### The Family Takes Turns Answering

Once a family has control, the family members take turns giving answers to the same survey question. Each family can pick the order: strongest player first? Weakest last? They decide.

Each correct answer (an answer on the board) wins the family points equal to the number of people in the survey who said it. The family keeps guessing until:
- They get **three strikes** (three wrong guesses), OR
- They reveal **all the answers** on the board (a "sweep").

### Strikes: Three and You're Out

Every time a player gives an answer that is NOT on the board, they get a **strike**. Visual strike markers (like an X, a red mark, or a buzzer sound) appear on screen. After the third strike, the controlling family's turn ends immediately, and they lose control.

### The Steal: One Last Chance

After the controlling family gets three strikes, the other family (the "steal team") can confer (discuss briefly), then gives **one answer**—any of the remaining unrevealed answers on the board. 

- **If the steal guess is correct:** The sources indicate the steal team commonly wins all the points the first family had accumulated that round, plus the points for the stolen answer. (The stealing team takes over control for the next round.)
- **If the steal guess is wrong:** The original controlling team keeps all the points they earned that round, and they stay in control for the next round.

### Round Scoring

Each answer's points are the number of people in the survey who gave it. The sources confirm that as the game progresses, point values are eventually doubled and then tripled in later rounds, while the number of answers on the board shrinks. However, the exact rounds where these multipliers apply are not explicitly confirmed in the verified sources.

After each round, the host announces the cumulative score for each family.

### Invented Example: A Complete Round

**Setup (Normal Points, No Multiplier):**
- Question: "Name something in your kitchen"
- Board has 5 answers: Fridge (30 pts), Stove (26 pts), Sink (20 pts), Microwave (12 pts), Toaster (4 pts)

**Face-Off:** 
- Red Team's Alice buzzes in first and says "Fridge"—it's #1! Red Team wins the face-off.
- Red Team chooses to **PLAY.**

**Red Team Plays (in order: Bob, Charlie, Diana, Evan):**
- Fridge is revealed: Red Team's pile = **30 points**
- Bob guesses "Stove" → Correct! Pile = 30 + 26 = **56 points**
- Charlie guesses "Freezer" → Not on the board. **Strike #1.**
- Diana guesses "Sink" → Correct! Pile = 56 + 20 = **76 points**
- Evan guesses "Oven" → Not on the board. **Strike #2.**
- Back to Bob: guesses "Cabinet" → Not on the board. **Strike #3. Red Team loses control.**

**Red Team's Round Total so far: 76 points**

**Blue Team Steals:**
- Blue Team confers and guesses: "Microwave!"
- Host reveals: "Microwave is on the board!"
- Blue Team successfully steals! They win 76 points (Red's pile) + 12 points (Microwave) = **88 points for this round.**
- Blue Team takes control of the next round.

**Alternate Scenario (Failed Steal):**
- If Blue Team had guessed "Oven" (not on the board) instead of "Microwave":
- The steal fails. Red Team keeps their 76 points for the round.
- Red Team remains in control for the next round.

**Round Summary:**
- **Successful steal:** Blue Team: 88 points, Red Team: 0 for this round
- **Failed steal:** Red Team: 76 points, Blue Team: 0 for this round

### Winning Score and Sudden Death

The **target is 300 points.** The first family to reach 300 wins the main game and advances to Fast Money. If neither family reaches 300 after four rounds, the game goes to **sudden death**: one final face-off question (worth 3× points, top answer only) determines the winner. The first family to buzz in with the #1 answer wins.

## Fast Money: The Bonus Round

After winning the main game, the winning family selects two of its members to play Fast Money.

### How Fast Money Works

**Player 1** sits on stage with a timer visible. The host reads a question out loud. Player 1 has **20 seconds** to answer as many of the five questions as possible. Each answer must match one from the 100-person survey to earn points. Player 1 earns points equal to the number of survey respondents who gave that answer (e.g., if 40 people said "red" when asked to name a color, Player 1 scores 40 points).

Player 1 can **pass** on a question and skip to the next one. If time remains after answering all five questions, Player 1 can return to any passed questions.

**Player 2** then goes to a soundproof booth (or private area off-stage) so they can't hear Player 1's answers. The host asks the exact same five questions, and Player 2 has **25 seconds.** Player 2 earns points the same way—1 point per survey respondent per answer.

**IMPORTANT:** Player 2 **cannot repeat any of Player 1's answers.** If Player 2 accidentally says the same answer, a buzzer sounds and the host says "Try Again."—Player 2 must give a different answer. The duplicate answer scores zero.

### The Target and Prize

The two players' scores are added together. If the **combined total is 200 or more points**, the family wins the **grand prize: $20,000** (or $50,000 for special anniversary episodes). If they score below 200 points, they receive **$5 for each point** they earned (e.g., 150 points = $750).

### Invented Example: Fast Money

**First Player (Jade, 20 seconds):**

| Question | Jade's Answer | Points |
|----------|---------------|--------|
| Name a color | Blue | 25 points |
| Name a pizza topping | Pepperoni | 18 points |
| Name a state | California | 30 points |
| Name a movie genre | Action | 9 points |
| Name a holiday | Christmas | 22 points |

**Jade's Total: 25 + 18 + 30 + 9 + 22 = 104 points**

**Second Player (Marcus, 25 seconds, cannot repeat Jade's answers):**

| Question | Marcus's Answer | Points | Notes |
|----------|-----------------|--------|-------|
| Name a color | Red | 21 points | (Not Blue) |
| Name a pizza topping | Cheese | 33 points | (Not Pepperoni) |
| Name a state | Passes, returns later but still misses | 0 points | Can't think of one; tries again but doesn't match |
| Name a movie genre | Comedy | 17 points | (Not Action) |
| Name a holiday | Easter | 40 points | (Not Christmas) |

**Marcus's Total: 21 + 33 + 0 + 17 + 40 = 111 points**

**Family Total: 104 + 111 = 215 points**

**Result:** The family wins **$20,000** because they scored 215 points, well above the 200-point target.

**Note:** Individual survey answers are usually well under 50 points each because the survey only has 100 people, so a combined Fast Money total of 200 is already a strong result.

## What the Sources Do Not Settle

The research notes identify several gaps where the TV rules are either ambiguous or not publicly documented:

- **Which rounds have which multipliers:** The verified sources confirm that point values are "eventually doubled and then tripled" as the game progresses, but do not explicitly state which specific rounds have which multiplier. The exact pattern and which round is the first to use multipliers is not confirmed.

- **Steal points distribution in the current (2010+) era:** The verified sources confirm that a successful steal means one answer attempt from the remaining hidden ones, but the sources state that the stealing team received all accumulated points "from 1999 until 2003." Steve Harvey's era began in 2010, well after this documented period. Whether the current (post-2010) rule keeps the full-pile distribution is commonly reported but not explicitly confirmed in the verified sources for the Harvey era.

- **Duplicate answer rule on the main board:** The Fast Money duplicate rule (buzzer, "Try Again") is confirmed. However, the research notes do not confirm whether an answer already revealed on the main board of the TV show costs a strike or is handled differently. (For this event, we've clarified the house rule: it shows "ALREADY ON THE BOARD" and costs neither points nor a strike.)

- **"Close enough" answer rulings:** The host has discretion to accept answers that are semantically similar to board answers (e.g., "car" and "automobile"), but there is no published rubric. The host's judgment is final.

- **Buzzer mechanics:** The TV show mentions contestants "buzz in," but does not detail the specific technology, false-start penalties, or hand behavior.

## How to Run a Live Version: Roles, Equipment, and Tips

### Roles to Assign (Minimum Setup)

1. **Host:** Reads questions aloud, reveals answers, announces scores, keeps energy high, makes calls on disputed answers. Needs a microphone if the room is larger than 20 people. This person should be confident, clear, and comfortable making snap judgments.

2. **Scorekeeper:** Tracks each team's cumulative score, strike count (reset each round), and current round points. Can use paper, a whiteboard, or a digital board. This person must listen carefully and do basic math.

3. **Answer-Board Operator:** Controls which answers are visible. If using a physical board, they reveal/cover answers as guesses are made. If using a screen, they control the display.

4. **Timekeeper:** (Optional but helpful for Fast Money) Keeps a stopwatch or timer visible, calls time when it's up.

5. **Judge/Dispute Resolver:** (Optional, but recommended for 50+ spectators) Settles arguments over whether an answer is "close enough."

### Equipment You'll Need

- **Buzzers or a buzzer app:** A hand bell for each team, two phone buzzer apps, or a laptop key assigned to each team.
- **Answer board:** A whiteboard, poster board, or digital screen. Must be readable from the back of the room. Test visibility before the event.
- **Timer:** A visible countdown timer (can be on a phone, projected on screen, or a physical timer).
- **Scoreboard:** A visible board or screen where each team's score is updated live.
- **Microphone:** Essential if more than 20 people. Use a handheld or lapel mic; speakers should face the audience, not the stage.
- **Questions and survey data:** Prepare 3–6 questions with the answers already ranked. (See below for how to prepare survey data.)
- **Optional soundboard:** A laptop or tablet with sound effects (ding for correct, buzzer for wrong, theme music). Many find sound effects really increase the energy.

### How to Prepare Survey Questions and Answers

For each question, you'll need:

1. A survey of 20–50 people (staff, pre-event volunteers, or a quick crowd poll) answering the same question.
2. Tally the responses by frequency.
3. Rank them: most common answers first, least common last.
4. Keep only the top 5–8 answers (or fewer if few unique answers were given).
5. Each answer is worth points equal to the number of people who gave it.

**Example setup:**
- Question: "Name a type of weather"
- Survey results (30 people asked):
  - Rain: 10 people → 10 points
  - Sunny: 8 people → 8 points
  - Snow: 7 people → 7 points
  - Cloudy: 3 people → 3 points
  - Windy: 2 people → 2 points
- Board display: Show these five in order.

### A Suggested Run Sheet and Timing

**Total event time: 30–60 minutes** (adjust for your audience)

| Phase | Time | Notes |
|-------|------|-------|
| **Setup & Explain Rules** | 5 min | Divide into two teams, assign roles, explain face-off and strikes. Keep it brief. |
| **Main Game: Round 1** | 8 min | One face-off, host picks starting team or uses a toss-up, 2–3 minutes of play, one potential steal. Aim for ~3 min per question. |
| **Main Game: Round 2** | 8 min | Same structure. |
| **Main Game: Round 3** | 8 min | Same structure. |
| **Announce Winner** | 2 min | Host celebrates the winning team. |
| **Fast Money (Optional)** | 10 min | Winning team's two best players take turns. Each gets 15–20 seconds, or play on paper/by voice. |
| **Closing & Prizes** | 2 min | Awards, photos, thank-yous. |

**Pacing tips:**
- If a team is taking too long to think (>20 seconds), call a strike and move on. (house suggestion)
- Keep spectators engaged: encourage them to shout out answers or make predictions during breaks.
- Use sound effects (ding, buzzer, theme music) to punctuate moments and maintain energy.

### How to Judge Answers Fairly

An answer counts if:
1. It **exactly matches** a board answer, OR
2. It is **semantically equivalent** (same meaning, different wording).

**Examples of acceptable:** 
- "Car" and "automobile" → same category, both count
- "Coca-Cola" and "Coke" → common variations, both count
- "Television" and "TV" → abbreviation, counts

**Examples of NOT acceptable:**
- Repeating an already-revealed answer (in our event: "ALREADY ON THE BOARD")
- A brand when the category is general (e.g., "Chevy" instead of "car")
- A completely different idea

**If there's a dispute:**
- Host makes the judgment call.
- If a judge is assigned, host and judge confer briefly and rule.
- **Err on the side of fun:** This isn't a court of law. If an answer is close and the crowd likes it, accept it.

### Common First-Timer Mistakes

1. **Not testing audio and sight lines before the event.** 30 minutes before start time, stand at the back of the room and check:
   - Can you hear the host clearly? Can people in the back hear?
   - Can you read every word on the board? If not, make the text bigger.
   - Are speakers pointed at the audience (not the stage)?

2. **Choosing a host who isn't comfortable on stage.** Pick someone who is confident, speaks clearly, and can react with humor. Practice the rules with them first.

3. **Running too many rounds without breaks.** If you have a large audience watching, they get bored. Alternate between main-game rounds and audience activities (see below).

4. **Not explaining the rules clearly.** Spend 3 minutes at the start explaining face-off, strikes, and steal. Most people have never played.

5. **Losing spectators to boredom.** If 100 people are watching and only 10 are playing, the 90 will tune out. Use audience-participation mechanics (see below).

### Keeping the Room Excited: Energy, Sound, and Audience Participation

**Host Energy:**
- Use big gestures, comedy, and enthusiasm. Comment on surprising answers.
- Build drama: "They have two strikes… one answer left on the board… will they guess it?"
- Celebrate good plays and tease the losing team (lightheartedly).

**Sound Effects & Music (Optional but Powerful):**
- **Opening theme:** Play upbeat music as the game starts.
- **"Survey Says!" moment:** Play a short sound bite as the first answer is revealed.
- **Correct answer ding:** A pleasant chime or "ding" sound.
- **Wrong answer buzzer:** A buzzer or "BZZZT" sound when a strike occurs.
- **Transition music:** Brief music between rounds.

**Note on music:** Do NOT copy the TV show's copyrighted theme music. Instead, use royalty-free music libraries (check each licence for terms of use). Choose upbeat, game-show-style music.

**Audience Participation (House Additions, Not TV Rules):**

These are optional add-ons to keep spectators engaged:

1. **Crowd Polls & Phone Voting:** Ask the room what the team should say. The host types the two to six loudest guesses. Phones vote on which guess the room likes best, and the most popular one is shown on the big screen as a suggestion. The team may use it or ignore it. This is a house suggestion, not a TV rule. In our app this is the crowd-assist poll, and its choices are always the room's guesses, never the hidden answers.

2. **Audience Stealing:**
   - When a team gets three strikes and the other team fails to steal, open it to the audience.
   - Shout: "Audience! What's the next answer?" 
   - First person to shout the correct answer wins a small prize.

3. **Predictions:**
   - Before each round, ask spectators to predict which team will win.
   - Keep a tally; the person with the most correct predictions wins a prize at the end.

4. **Crowd Cheering:**
   - Encourage spectators to cheer for their favorite team.
   - Use audience energy as fuel for the game's momentum.

## Glossary of Terms

**Answer:** A response a survey respondent gave to the question. Only answers given by 2+ people appear on the board.

**Board:** The display (physical or digital) showing all the answers to the current question and their point values.

**Buzzer:** The device (or app) each team uses to signal they want to answer during the face-off.

**Control:** The right to keep answering questions; held by the family that wins the face-off or successfully steals.

**Face-Off:** The opening head-to-head moment where one person from each team races to give the first answer.

**Fast Money:** The bonus round after the main game where the winning family's two best players answer rapid-fire questions for up to $20,000.

**Grand Prize:** The full reward for reaching the Fast Money target (200+ points in the original format), typically $20,000.

**Multiplier:** A factor applied to each answer's points in later rounds to increase their value. The TV show uses multipliers in later rounds, but the exact structure is not confirmed. (If you choose to use multipliers; our app does not.)

**Pass:** The choice a team makes to give up control and let the other team play instead.

**Steal:** The opportunity for the defending team to answer one remaining question if the controlling team gets three strikes. A successful steal wins all points accumulated that round.

**Strike:** A penalty for giving an answer not on the board. Three strikes and the team loses control.

**Survey:** The 100-person poll (or 20–50 person poll for live events) conducted before the game where people answer each question. Their responses determine which answers appear on the board and how many points each is worth.

**Sweep:** Revealing all answers on the board without getting three strikes (rarely happens but possible).

## Event Context: GDG Family Feud on the App (Tuesday, 6 October 2026)

**This event uses a custom app. Below are the rules as they appear in the app:**

**(a) Team Setup:** Two teams of any size you choose.

**(b) Rounds:** Three rounds by default. The moderator can change the number of rounds if needed.

**(c) Scoring:** No multipliers. Every round is normal value. Each answer scores the survey count supplied by the events team (until real survey results arrive, the app uses clearly labelled invented demo answers for testing).

**(d) How a Round Works:**
1. Host introduces the question and the category.
2. The answer board is hidden.
3. The host picks which team starts (for example, by a coin toss, a quick toss-up question, or host decision). There is no face-off step in the app; the host decides.
4. The team guesses aloud.
5. The host judges whether the answer matches one on the board.
   - If it matches: Host presses **Reveal** (the answer appears, points go to the team's pile).
   - If it doesn't match: Host presses **Add strike** (the team gets a strike, pile stays the same).
6. The team keeps answering until three strikes.
7. After strike 3: The other team gets one steal guess. If correct: they get the whole pile (including the stolen answer's points). If wrong: the first team keeps the pile.
8. Host presses **Award** to finish the round and move to the next one.

**(e) Repeated Answers:** If a team guesses an answer already on the board, the app shows **"ALREADY ON THE BOARD"** and costs the team neither points nor a strike.

**(f) After the Award:** The remaining unrevealed answers can be shown for fun, but they score no points.

**(g) Winner:** The team with more points after the last round. A tie is allowed.

**(h) Corrections:** The app has Undo and score-correction buttons if the host makes a mistake.

**(i) Crowd-Assist Poll (Optional):** During a team's turn, the host types 2–6 guesses the room is shouting. Phones vote on which guess the room likes best. The poll result is only a suggestion for the team—it never reveals a hidden answer or scores points. The poll options are the host's typed guesses, never the hidden answers.

**(j) Fast Money:** The app does not include Fast Money. If you want to play it, use paper or run it by voice.
